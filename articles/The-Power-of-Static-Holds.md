## Quick Answer

Static holds, also known as isometric exercises, build strength by creating muscular tension without joint movement. A systematic review of isometric training found substantial improvements in muscle size and maximal force production regardless of training intensity, and holds at longer muscle lengths carried over better to dynamic performance (Oranchuk et al., 2019). Tendons are a different story: the same review found that high-intensity contractions were required to improve tendon structure and function, so an easy plank alone shouldn't be expected to change your tendons. Static holds also need no equipment, which makes them easy to repeat daily.

## What Static Holds Are

Static holds are **isometric contractions**: positions where you generate force without moving your joints.

Examples:

- Plank
- Wall sit
- Hollow body hold
- L-sit
- Handstand hold
- Tuck planche
- Front lever hold

During a static hold:

- Your muscles contract without changing length
- Your joints stay fixed
- Your stabilizing muscles work continuously

Holds range from beginner-friendly (a plank, a wall sit) to very demanding (a tuck planche, a front lever). That range matters, as the research below shows.

## The Science Behind Static Holds

A 2019 systematic review of isometric training, covering studies of at least three weeks, reached several findings (Oranchuk et al., 2019):

- **Strength and size.** Substantial improvements in muscle size (hypertrophy) and maximal force production were reported regardless of training intensity.
- **Muscle length matters.** Isometric training at longer muscle lengths produced greater hypertrophy than equal volumes of training at shorter lengths, and showed greater transfer to dynamic performance.
- **Tendons need intensity.** High-intensity contractions, defined in the review as 70% or more, were required to improve tendon structure and function.

The authors also note that relatively few studies met the review's inclusion criteria, so this is an area where the evidence is still developing.

### What That Means in Practice

Easy holds you could maintain for minutes are good for building the habit and basic endurance. If you want to challenge your tendons, the review points toward harder holds that you can only maintain briefly, the demanding end of the progressions.

That last step is our inference from the review's findings, not a prescription. Progress gradually, and let your form set the limit.

## Static Holds and Posture

Posture is shaped by many things: daily habits, your work setup, mobility, and strength.

A 2024 meta-analysis found that therapeutic exercise programs, using strengthening, stretching, and shoulder-based exercises, improved forward head posture, rounded shoulders, and thoracic kyphosis in people with upper crossed syndrome (Sepehri et al., 2024).

Those were structured corrective programs, not static holds on their own, so we can't promise that holds fix posture. What holds do give you is a simple way to train your trunk and shoulder girdle to maintain a position under tension. That makes them a useful piece of a broader program.

## Static Holds and Weight Training: Complements, Not Competitors

Weight training moves a load through a range of motion. Static holds train you to produce force at a fixed position. They are different tools.

Isometric training can build strength and muscle (Oranchuk et al., 2019), but how well that carries over to dynamic movement depends on how you train. Most people get the best results from a mix of both, not one or the other.

For calisthenics skills like handstands, levers, and planche progressions, static holds are also the skill itself: you practice the position you want to own.

## Five Benefits of Static Holds

### 1. Strength and Muscle Without Equipment

Isometric training produced substantial gains in muscle size and maximal force in the studies reviewed (Oranchuk et al., 2019).

### 2. Better Carryover at Longer Muscle Lengths

Holds at longer muscle lengths showed greater transfer to dynamic performance (Oranchuk et al., 2019).

### 3. A Way to Challenge Your Tendons

High-intensity holds are the ones associated with tendon adaptations (Oranchuk et al., 2019), so progressing to harder variations has a purpose.

### 4. A Useful Piece of a Posture Program

Strengthening and shoulder work are components of exercise programs shown to improve posture measures (Sepehri et al., 2024). Holds are an easy way to include that work.

### 5. A Simple, Repeatable Routine

Simple behaviors become habits faster (Lally et al., 2010), and holds are about as simple as training gets.

## Common Mistakes

### Holding With Poor Form

A hold with poor form means you're practicing a poor position. End the hold when your form breaks down.

### Holding Too Long Too Soon

Start small. Build gradually.

### Holding Your Breath

Breathe steadily throughout a hold. Holding your breath under strain can temporarily raise blood pressure, so if you have a heart or blood pressure condition, talk to your doctor before attempting hard holds.

### Skipping Dynamic Training

Holds complement dynamic exercise. Doing only one limits what you train.
